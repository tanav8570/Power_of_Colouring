The power of colouring

Take a puzzle you cannot solve. Colour it in. Count the colours. Surprisingly often, the answer is then sitting in front of you. This is a tour of that one idea, from a board of tiles to two questions that nobody on Earth can answer yet.

A puzzle with no way in

Here is a 10 × 10 board and a pile of strips, each covering four squares in a straight line. 100 squares, 25 strips: the arithmetic says they should fit. Try it.

You will reach 24 strips and stall, with four squares that refuse to line up. But failing a thousand times proves nothing. Maybe the right arrangement is one you have not tried.

Switch on Chessboard, the colouring everyone tries first. It fails: 50 squares of each colour, every strip covers two of each, and nothing is contradicted. The colouring is correct, and blind.

Now switch on Diagonals, 4 colours. A strip walking four steps in a straight line crosses four diagonals in a row, so wherever it lies it covers exactly one blue, one orange, one green and one pink square. 25 strips would need 25 of each. The board has 25, 26, 25 and only 24 pink. The puzzle is impossible, and we never examined a single arrangement.

The method in three steps. Colour the board so that every piece is forced to take the same share of each colour. Count the colours on the board. If the board's share is different, no arrangement can ever work.

How I got hooked

I met this idea on an olympiad problem sheet about grids. My teacher handed us problems before teaching the method, so nobody told me to colour anything. I only knew that the squares were all identical, and identical squares give you nothing to hold on to. Colouring is a way of giving them names.

My first attempt was the chessboard, and it told me nothing. What I enjoyed was that the failure pointed to what I needed: a pattern that a strip cannot treat evenly. Blocks of 2 × 2 work (52 against 48). Diagonals work best, because you can see why at a glance. Later I met a problem where giving diametrically opposite points the same colour cracked it open, and I began to suspect the idea was much bigger than grids.

It is. The same move decides how many guards a museum needs, finds three mutual friends or strangers at any party of six, splits rent fairly, and sits inside two problems that nobody has solved.

Part one: colouring proves that something is impossible

The oldest trick on the board

Cut two diagonally opposite corners off a chessboard. Can 31 dominoes cover the 62 squares that are left?

Here the plain chessboard is exactly the right colouring. A domino always covers one dark and one light square. Opposite corners share a colour, so removing them leaves 30 of one and 32 of the other, and 31 dominoes need 31 of each.

My four-colour diagonals would be useless here: a domino covers two neighbouring diagonals, whose colours change as it moves, so it takes no fixed share. The colouring has to fit the piece. Remove one dark and one light square instead, anywhere, and the page always finds a tiling. That is a theorem of Ralph Gomory. Solomon Golomb turned arguments like this into a subject of their own in 1954.1

Where counting goes blind

If the colours balance, must a tiling exist?

The corner square has lost both its neighbours, so no domino can ever reach it, yet the colours balance perfectly. A colour count is a necessary condition, never a sufficient one. When the count fails, you have a proof. When it passes, you have only learned that this colouring cannot see the problem. Catching the stuck corner needs a different idea: pairing each dark square with a light neighbour, and noticing that the corner has nobody to pair with.

Go deeper: colours that are numbers

A colouring is just a label on each square that keeps some total fixed, and the labels can be numbers. In Conway's Soldiers, pegs fill every square below a line and move by peg-solitaire jumps. John Conway gave each square a weight built from the golden ratio, so that no jump can increase the total. Everything below the line adds up to exactly the weight of one square five rows up, and you only ever have finitely many pegs. So no peg can reach row five.

A knight has two colours

Whatever a knight does, it lands on the opposite colour.

So after an odd number of moves it is on the wrong colour and cannot be home. One sentence rules out every odd route at once. Colouring by rows would fail: a knight moves one or two rows, so the row colour sometimes flips and sometimes does not. The chessboard is the colouring that flips every time. A consequence: a knight can never visit every square of a 5 × 5 board and return to its start, because that round trip takes 25 moves.

Does my trick always work?

I wanted to know whether the diagonals were a lucky fit for 10 × 10. So take any board and strips of any length k, and colour the diagonals with k repeating colours.

If k divides a side, the strips line up and the colours are exactly equal. Otherwise the colours come out unequal, and the board is impossible. There is no third case:

A rectangle can be covered by 1 × k strips exactly when k divides one of its sides. Counting area alone cannot see this: 4 divides 100, yet 10 × 10 fails.

This is a theorem of N. G. de Bruijn and David Klarner from 1969, and the colouring I had stumbled on is its standard proof.2 I checked every board up to 12 × 12 by computer before I believed it.

Go deeper: where the diagonals fail and the chessboard wins

Can T-shaped pieces of four squares cover a 10 × 10 board? Diagonal colouring says nothing, because a T takes no fixed share of the diagonals. The chessboard does: a T always covers three squares of one colour and one of the other, an odd number of dark squares. 25 pieces give an odd total, but the board has 50 dark squares. Impossible. Choosing the colouring to suit the piece is the real skill.

Rectangles with a whole-number side

A large rectangle is cut into smaller ones, and each small rectangle has at least one side of whole-number length. Must the large rectangle have one too? Nothing here sits on a grid, so where would the colours go? The trick is to colour the plane itself, as a chessboard of half-unit squares.

Two facts do all the work. A rectangle with a whole-number side is balanced wherever it sits, because every unit of length is half one colour and half the other. A rectangle cornered at (0, 0) is balanced only if a side is whole. Now pin the large rectangle to the corner. Its pieces are balanced, so it is balanced, so one of its sides is whole.

The size of the squares matters. With ordinary unit squares the argument collapses: a 1 × 1 square at the corner is entirely one colour, so a whole-number side no longer means balanced. The colouring must be built to detect exactly the property you care about. Stan Wagon collected fourteen proofs of this theorem;3 this is the one I would show a friend.

Part two: colouring counts things

Necklaces and a theorem from 1640

Colour 5 beads in a row, blue or orange: 32 ways. Join the ends into a necklace, and strings that are rotations of each other become the same necklace.

The two single-colour necklaces stand alone, and every other one stands for exactly 5 strings. So 32 − 2 = 30 splits into groups of 5. Why exactly 5? A necklace that looks the same after a smaller turn must repeat in blocks, and the block length would divide 5. A prime has no such divisors. With 4 or 6 beads the argument fails in front of you: patterns like blue-orange-blue-orange repeat early and form smaller groups.

Fermat's little theorem. If p is prime, then ap − a is divisible by p. Colour p beads with a colours, set aside the a single-colour necklaces, and the rest fall into groups of exactly p.

Fermat stated it in 1640. This bead proof was published by Golomb in 1956.4 The theorem now sits underneath the encryption that protects online payments.

Part three: colouring proves that something must exist

Guarding a gallery

A gallery has straight walls and n corners. Guards stand still and see in every direction, but not through walls. How many guards are always enough?

The obvious approach is to cut the floor into triangles and put a guard in each one. That needs n − 2 guards, far too many. Steve Fisk's colouring does three times better.5 Colour the corners with three colours so that every triangle gets one of each. This is always possible: neighbouring triangles share two corners, which forces the colour of the third. Now every triangle has a corner of every colour, so guards on any one colour watch the whole floor. Three colours share n corners, so the rarest has at most n ÷ 3.

The comb shows this cannot be improved: each of its four spikes needs a guard of its own. Notice the change of direction. In part one, colouring proved that no arrangement exists. Here it builds one, for every gallery that could ever be drawn.

Six people at a party

Any two people at a party are either friends or strangers. Among any six, there are always three mutual friends or three mutual strangers. Colour each link pink for friends and blue for strangers: the claim is that a one-colour triangle is unavoidable.

Brute force confirms it, since none of the 32,768 colourings escapes, but it explains nothing. The colouring argument takes three lines. Asha has five links in only two colours, so three of them match, say pink to Bilal, Chen and Divya. If any two of those three are friends, they make a pink triangle with Asha. If none are, the three of them are a blue triangle.

With five people the argument breaks, because Asha has only four links and they can split two and two. Press Show the escape for 5: a pink pentagon with a blue star inside. My search found exactly 12 escapes among 1,024 colourings, all this same pattern placed differently. This is the first step of Ramsey theory, whose message, in Theodore Motzkin's words, is that complete disorder is impossible.

Colouring by coin flip

Six people force a triangle; 18 force four mutual friends or strangers. How fast do these numbers grow? To show that a big party can avoid a large one-colour group, you must produce a colouring, and building one by hand fails badly. In 1947 Paul Erdős stopped building. He flipped a coin for every link.6

Six people, groups of four. There are 15 groups, and each is one colour only if its six links all agree: a chance of 1 in 32. So a random colouring has 15 ÷ 32 ≈ 0.47 one-colour groups on average. But each colouring has a whole number of them. If the average is below 1, some colouring has exactly 0. It is like a league averaging under one goal a match: some match ended goalless.

The power shows at scale. For 1,000 people and groups of 20 the average is about 0.0000000000000004, so a clean colouring certainly exists, and almost every random one works. Yet nobody can write one down: no known recipe builds colourings nearly as good as the ones coins find. This is the probabilistic method, now one of the main tools of combinatorics, and the strangest use of colouring on this page. We do not even choose the colours.

A triangle that must have a rainbow

A big triangle is cut into small ones, and every dot gets one of three colours. Two rules: the big corners get three different colours, and a dot on a side may only use the two colours at the ends of that side. Inside, anything goes.

Some small triangle always gets all three colours. In fact the number of them is always odd. This is Sperner's lemma, from 1928. Call an edge with one blue end and one orange end a door. Enter through a door on the bottom side. A room with all three colours has exactly one door; every other room has none or two. So a walk through doors either ends in a rainbow room or comes back out through the bottom. The bottom side runs from blue to orange, so it has an odd number of doors. Walks that come back out use them in pairs, so one walk is left over, and it ends in a rainbow.

The side rule is what makes this work. Break it, by colouring every dot blue except the three corners, and there is no rainbow anywhere.

Splitting the rent

Three friends rent a flat with three different rooms. Can the rent always be split so that nobody envies anyone? Francis Su showed it can.7 Each point of the triangle is one way to split the rent. Each dot is handed to one friend, who answers: at these prices, which room would you take? The answer is the dot's colour. A rainbow triangle is three almost identical price splits where three different people want three different rooms. Shrink the triangles and you have prices that make everyone content.

Part four: colouring in the wild

Colouring you already do

Draw a dot for each exam, join two exams if some student takes both, and treat time slots as colours. A clash-free timetable is a colouring in which joined dots never match.

Three slots always fail. Algebra, Physics, Programming, Probability and Chemistry form a ring of five, a ring of odd length needs three colours, and Calculus clashes with all five, so it needs a fourth. Maps (four colours always suffice: guessed in 1852, proved by computer in 1976), Sudoku (81 cells, nine colours) and mobile-tower frequencies are the same problem. Deciding whether two colours suffice is easy: alternate. Deciding whether three suffice is believed to be genuinely hard, and is tied to the P versus NP problem.

In 1989 Geoffrey Exoo found a colouring of 42 people with no one-colour group of five. In 2024 Vigleik Angeltveit and Brendan McKay proved, with a large double-checked computer search, that 46 always force one.8 Brute force cannot close the gap: 43 people have 903 links, so 2903 colourings, a number with 272 digits. Erdős joked that if aliens demanded this number on pain of destroying Earth, we should set every mathematician and computer to work on it.

Colouring the whole plane

In 1950 Edward Nelson, then 18, asked: colour every point of the plane so that points exactly 1 apart never match. How few colours can do it? Seven are enough, using hexagons a little under 1 across, so no two points in one tile are 1 apart, and tiles of the same colour sit more than 1 apart.

Three colours fail, and the proof is seven points joined by eleven links of length exactly 1, the Moser spindle.

So the answer was between 4 and 7 for almost seventy years. In 2018 Aubrey de Grey, a biologist who worked on it as a hobby, found 1,581 points that four colours cannot handle.9 The answer is 5, 6 or 7, and nobody knows which. A better pattern would lower the 7; a cleverer set of points would raise the 5. Both are searches for a colouring.

One idea, and a puzzle for you

On this page colouring counted and found a mismatch (strips, dominoes, rectangles), tracked what cannot change (the knight), sorted objects into equal groups (necklaces), built a solution (guards), and forced a pattern to exist (parties, rainbows, coin flips). The colours are never in the problem. We add them, and choosing them is where the creativity lies. My first chessboard colouring of the strip puzzle was not wrong. It was just looking at the wrong thing.

I will end the way my teacher began, with a problem and no method. An 8 × 8 board has one corner removed. Can 21 strips of three cover the other 63 squares?

A hint, if you want one

One direction of diagonals gives a condition on the missing square, and the other direction gives a second. Only four squares pass both. Colouring cannot tell you whether those four really work, so for them you have to find a tiling yourself.
